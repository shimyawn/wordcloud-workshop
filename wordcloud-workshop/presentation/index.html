1 · The method

A word cloud makes one promise: bigger means more often.

  • The mood at a glance. A whole text shrinks to one picture you read in seconds: an overview first.
  • Surprises jump out. A large or brightly coloured word is found before you search for it: popout, processed in parallel.
  • No axis to learn. The mark is the word itself, and its size is a magnitude channel anyone reads as "a lot".
Introduce the vocabulary we use all session: a mark is the thing drawn (here, a word), a channel is how we vary it (size, colour, position).

1 · The method

It also has three blind spots.

  • Position means nothing. The most effective channel, position, is spent on packing: words go wherever they fit.
  • Long words look more important. "excellent" covers more area than "bad" at the same count. Area mixes word length with frequency.
  • Cut words leave no trace. A long tail of rare words must be cut, and the reader cannot see what was left out or how much.

Our cloud keeps the promise and answers two of them: exact counts sit in a tooltip, and every cloud states how many words it hides. Task 2 answers the third.

The three blind spots return later: step 4 prints what it hides, step 6 adds details on demand, Task 2 gives position a job.

2 · The data

Most words appear only once.

58% of the 5,069 distinct words appear in a single sentence. This is a long tail (Zipf's law).

26 = 26 Rank 100 and rank 101 appear in the same number of sentences. A "top 100" cut splits a tie.

Consequence one: no cloud shows every word. Consequence two: if we cut, a rule is fairer than a rank. Task 2 lets them see the tie problem live.

2 · The data

"not" changes the word after it.

Counted word by word, "not good" makes good look less positive than it is.

Counted as one term, not good sits at 4% praise. And not disappointed at 100%.

In text analysis this is negation handling: a small rule in step 2.

This is why toTerms is not a one-liner. A bag of words loses the "not".

The idiom in three questions: what, why, how.

This is the analysis framework from the lectures. A word cloud is an idiom like any other: we can say what it shows, for which task, and how.

3 · Our design

Every channel answers to the data.

ChannelEncodesBecause
MarkA term, drawn as textText is the only mark that names itself; the reader needs the word
SizeSentences containing the term, square-root scale on a common scale for all cloudsA magnitude channel for a quantitative attribute. Font size squared ≈ area, so √ keeps area proportional to the count
ColourTone: green ≥ 70%, pink ≤ 30%, grey betweenA segmented diverging palette: the data has a meaningful midpoint (50%), so neutral grey there and saturated colours at the ends. Words are small marks, and small regions need high saturation
PositionBase: nothing, packing only. Task 2: horizontal position = share of praiseThe most effective channel. Unused in a classic cloud; in Task 2 a magnitude channel for share, and 500 : 500 makes the centre exactly neutral
ItalicA "not …" phrase counted as one termMarks a joined phrase without spending another colour
ShownEvery term in at least 7 sentences of its source7 / 0 gives 71%: fewer sentences can never reach green or pink

After Task 2, share drives both colour and horizontal position: a redundant encoding. It sends a stronger message, and the reader no longer depends on hue alone, which also helps colour-blind readers.

Expressiveness: magnitude channels (size, position) for quantitative attributes; colour for the ordered tone. Effectiveness: position is the most accurate channel, so it goes to the main attribute, share.

3 · Our design

Why 70%, 30% and 7 sentences, and not round numbers?

share = (praise + 5) ÷ (praise + complaint + 10)

Five imaginary sentences on each side pull small counts towards 50%: additive smoothing. A word seen 3 times, all in praise, gets 8 ÷ 13 = 62%, not 100%.

In this data the 70% and 30% cut-offs closely match a standard significance test for word use (log-odds, Monroe et al. 2008).

7 is not tuned: it is the smallest count where a one-sided word can reach a colour.

4 · The code · Key D3 piece 1 · d3.scaleSqrt

      
The size scale: from a count to pixels.

      

        
const size = d3.scaleSqrt()
  .domain([CONFIG.minCount, d3.max(selected.flatMap(s => s.kept), t => t.count)])
  .range(CONFIG.fontRange);
From Wordcloud_clean/wordcloud.js · lines that do not matter here are left out (…)

        

          
What it does
Turns a term's sentence count into a font size: 7 sentences → 10 px, the largest count → 40 px.

          
Why it is needed
Without a scale there is no rule from data to size. A square-root scale keeps the word's area proportional to its count; one scale for all three clouds keeps them comparable.

          
If removed or changed
With scaleLinear, area grows with the square of the count and big words swamp the cloud. With a separate scale per cloud, "film" and "food" would look equal even if their counts differ.

          
D3-specific
d3.scaleSqrt, .domain(), .range(), d3.max. The arrow functions and flatMap are plain JavaScript.

7 · Wrap-up

      
One cloud, two changes, two lessons.

      

        
Base: cut by a rule
A classic cloud. Every word in 7 sentences or more, the fewest that can lean, coloured by tone. The rule is written on the page, and so is what it hides.

        
Task 1: cut by a count
A top N is easy to explain. It draws an arbitrary line through tied words, and a large N fills the page with one-sentence evidence.

        
Task 2: give position a job
The same words, now grouped by side. Position is the most effective channel; in a classic cloud it is spent on packing.

      

      
Reduce and encode: what you leave out and which channel you spend are both design decisions.
